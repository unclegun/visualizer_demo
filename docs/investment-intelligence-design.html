Technical product case study · Working title

Market Compass

An incremental research platform that detects market conditions, measures historical outcomes, assembles point-in-time evidence, and helps investors inspect a thesis before making a decision.

Executive summary

A workbench for inspectable investment reasoning

The long-term thesis is simple: detect a meaningful condition, assemble relevant quantitative and qualitative evidence, compare the present with historical observations, and show what deserves deeper investigation. The system should answer what changed, why it may matter, what supports the idea, what contradicts it, and what would invalidate it—not collapse uncertainty into a magic score.

“Detect meaningful market conditions, assemble relevant evidence, compare the current situation with history, and help the investor decide what deserves deeper investigation.”

For the developer

A modular, testable path from Pine experiments to ASP.NET Core, PostgreSQL, deterministic analysis, temporal retrieval, and a citation-first research UI.

For the investor

A guided way to understand conditions, test hypotheses, record decisions before outcomes, and learn without confusing correlation with certainty.

Part 1

Pine Script v6 for a .NET developer

Pine Script v6 is TradingView’s cloud-based, domain-specific language for calculations on financial time series. It is designed for indicators, chart visualization, strategies and historical backtests, alerts, multi-timeframe analysis, condition detection, and reusable libraries. It is not a general application platform: databases, arbitrary file I/O, broad REST integration, distributed workflows, and full web applications belong in .NET or another general-purpose environment.

Traditional application

foreach (var candle in candles)
{
    Analyze(candle);
}

Explicit control flow: request → business logic → response.

Pine’s mental model

Bar → evaluate state → plot/signal

close, high, low, and volume are series whose value changes across chart bars.

close[1]    previous bar value
close[10]   value ten bars ago

This series-first execution model makes Pine an excellent learning and visualization environment. It lets a developer see whether an MA, RSI, volatility, or breakout calculation behaves sensibly on a chart before implementing a durable, testable equivalent in .NET.

Webhook design note: TradingView sends alert messages by HTTP POST and uses JSON content type when the body is valid JSON. The receiver should use HTTPS, authenticate the source, reject malformed or replayed events, create an idempotency key, persist/enqueue within the short response window, and monitor retries and dead letters. Part 2

Market concepts in developer language

This is the minimum financial vocabulary needed to understand the system. None of these measurements independently predicts the future.

Asset / security
Meaning: Something tradable; MSFT is company stock and SPY is an ETF tracking the S&P 500.
Developer analogy: The entity key around which observations are grouped.
Why it matters: Every bar, event, thesis, and decision needs an unambiguous instrument identity.
Index and benchmark
Meaning: An index measures a group of securities. The S&P 500 represents large U.S. companies; SPY is a commonly studied tradable proxy.
Developer analogy: A baseline integration test.
Why it matters: A +12% result underperformed if SPY returned +18%.
Price / OHLC
Meaning: Open, high, low, and close summarize a trading interval.
Developer analogy: A time-stamped record: { open, high, low, close, volume }.
Why it matters: It is the base time series for indicators, outcomes, and charts.
Volume
Meaning: The amount traded during an interval; it describes participation, not direction.
Developer analogy: Request throughput accompanying a state change.
Why it matters: A move with unusual participation may deserve different investigation than a thinly traded move.
Moving average
Meaning: A rolling mean over N observations. MA20, MA50, and MA200 approximate short, intermediate, and long horizons.
Developer analogy: A sliding-window aggregate.
Why it matters: Price above a rising MA200 is one explainable—not infallible—description of positive long-term trend.
Trend
Meaning: Whether general price direction is upward, downward, or flat over a stated horizon.
Developer analogy: The slope and state of a monitored metric.
Why it matters: Short- and long-term trends can disagree and create distinct historical conditions.
Momentum and RSI
Meaning: Momentum describes recent movement strength. RSI is a bounded momentum oscillator, not an automatic command.
Developer analogy: Rate-of-change telemetry normalized into a range.
Why it matters: It helps describe whether a move is accelerating, weakening, or extended.
Volatility and VIX
Meaning: Volatility measures the size and speed of price variation. VIX reflects option-implied expectations for S&P 500 volatility.
Developer analogy: Variance and instability, not positive or negative direction.
Why it matters: Risk conditions alter outcome distributions; universal VIX thresholds should be avoided.
Market breadth
Meaning: How many securities participate in a market move.
Developer analogy: Fleet health versus one large server’s health.
Why it matters: An index led by a few large companies differs from a broadly advancing market.
Relative strength
Meaning: Performance compared with another asset or benchmark—not RSI. If SPY is +8%, technology +12%, and MSFT +17%, MSFT leads both.
Developer analogy: Comparative performance against control groups.
Why it matters: It locates where strength or weakness is concentrated.
Return and drawdown
Meaning: Return is price change over a defined horizon; drawdown is decline from a previous peak (100 to 80 = −20%).
Developer analogy: Throughput gain plus worst degradation from peak capacity.
Why it matters: 1-day, 30-day, 90-day, and 1-year returns need risk context. Median is often less distorted than average by extremes.
Fundamentals
Meaning: Business performance: revenue, earnings, growth, free cash flow, margins, debt, and shares outstanding.
Developer analogy: Service internals rather than the market’s externally observed latency.
Why it matters: A stock price and the underlying company can tell different stories.
Valuation
Meaning: Price relative to business measures; P/E compares share price with earnings per share.
Developer analogy: Acquisition cost relative to generated capacity.
Why it matters: A great company is not automatically a good investment at every price.
Market regime
Meaning: An explainable classification such as Strong Bull, Bull, Cautious, Correction, Bear, or Recovery.
Developer analogy: A configuration-driven state machine.
Why it matters: The same signal can produce different outcome distributions in different environments.
Backtesting
Meaning: Apply fixed rules to historical data and observe how they would have behaved.
Developer analogy: Replay production traffic against an old release.
Why it matters: It generates evidence, but overfitting, look-ahead and survivorship bias, data quality, regime change, and costs can invalidate results.
Correlation versus causation
Meaning: “Rates fell and technology rose” does not prove that falling rates caused the rise.
Developer analogy: Correlated alerts do not establish a dependency graph.
Why it matters: The UI must label observed relationships, possible drivers, supporting evidence, and contradictions without inventing certainty.

Project principles

Evidence over prediction. Describe uncertainty; do not pretend to know the future.
Explain every signal. Every state must expose inputs and rules.
Point-in-time integrity. Historical analysis cannot use future knowledge.
Source everything. Material claims retain provenance and citations.
Separate data from interpretation. Code computes facts; LLMs explain supplied facts.
Record before outcomes. Immutable decision snapshots reduce hindsight bias.
Seek contradiction. Do not build a confirmation engine.
Complexity earns its place. Begin with a modular monolith and measurable needs.

Charting, indicators, news, fundamentals, and AI summaries already exist elsewhere. The potential differentiation is their combination with point-in-time retrieval, provenance, historical analogues, structured theses, immutable decisions, and outcome review: Git history + observability + hypothesis testing for investment decisions.

Parts 4 and 25

A modular monolith first

Use one ASP.NET Core deployable with strong module boundaries, PostgreSQL as the system of record, pgvector only when semantic retrieval arrives, and a .NET worker, Hangfire, or Quartz only after scheduling and retry requirements are known. Docker makes the runtime reproducible. Pine remains a laboratory and event source; Python is reserved for statistical workflows that are materially easier than C#.

Start the UI with Razor Pages and the repository’s existing HTMX patterns when server-rendered drill-downs are sufficient. Choose React only if the workbench develops dense client-side state, linked panels, or exploratory interactions that become awkward in server-rendered fragments; either UI should consume the same application contracts.

Point-in-time knowledge is an invariant

WHERE evidence.published_at <= :analysis_as_of
  AND evidence.available_at <= :analysis_as_of

If the question asks about MSFT on March 1, 2021, the system must exclude later filings, revisions, articles, and commentary. Track PublishedAt, EffectiveAt, AvailableAt, and RetrievedAt; use vintage-aware macro data such as ALFRED when revisions matter. Violating this rule creates look-ahead bias.

Parts 11–13

Thesis, decision, outcome, lesson

A thesis is structured data rather than a note. It contains a security, hypothesis, horizon, assumptions, risks, invalidation criteria, linked evidence, and status. New evidence is classified as supporting, contradicting, neutral, or unknown; the user remains responsible for interpretation.

MSFT thesis
Hypothesis: Azure and AI demand sustain above-market growth for 3–5 years.
Assumptions: cloud demand remains strong; AI monetization grows; margins endure.
Risks: capex returns disappoint; cloud growth slows; valuation becomes excessive.
Invalidation: define measurable conditions before the outcome is known.

A decision record captures the state of the world at that moment. Market, technical, fundamental, valuation, and evidence snapshots are immutable; corrections append new records rather than silently rewriting history.

1Decision

Reason and confidence recorded

2Outcome

Observed after defined horizon

3Review

Process separated from luck

4Lesson

Applied to future questions

Decision quality ≠ outcome quality. Track good decision + good outcome, good decision + bad outcome, bad decision + good outcome, and bad decision + bad outcome. Probabilistic reasoning can produce an unfavorable result; luck can reward a weak process.

Personal analytics can eventually surface recurring situations, concentration, thesis drift, momentum chasing, and overconfidence. Present them as observations until sample size and methodology justify stronger claims; never call them an investor’s “edge” prematurely.

Parts 29–31

Failure modes, tests, and operational evidence

Research risks

Overfitting, hindsight and survivorship bias, look-ahead bias, data snooping, small samples, regime change, misleading correlations, and ignored transaction costs.

Data risks

Outages, stale or revised data, adjusted/unadjusted confusion, corporate actions, symbol changes, duplicates, rate limits, licensing, and missing availability timestamps.

AI risks

Hallucination, causal invention, citation mismatch, omitted contradictions, temporal contamination, summarization loss, and confidence inflation.

Product risks

Appearing to give regulated personalized advice, presenting probability as certainty, encouraging blind score-following, opaque algorithms, and inadequate disclosures.

Obtain qualified legal and compliance advice before any commercial release involving personalized investment recommendations, licensed market data, or publisher content.
