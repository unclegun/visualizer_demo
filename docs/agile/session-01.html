Session 1

Agile, Scrum, and What Changes October 1

Why it matters Lead prep: 45–60 min Team session: 45–60 min
Why it matters: Teams need a shared mental model before the October 1 launch. Without it, they will treat Scrum as a new label for old work rather than a visible workflow for planning, review, and learning.

Agile vs Scrum

Agile is a broader mindset that values working software, customer feedback, and adaptation. Scrum is a specific framework that gives a team roles, time-boxed work, and a repeatable cadence.

ConceptAgileScrum
ScopeMindset and principlesSpecific framework
CadenceIterations can varyTime-boxed sprints
RolesMay be informalDefined roles with responsibilities
ArtifactsCan be looseBacklog, sprint backlog, increment

Transparency, inspection, adaptation

  • Transparency: visible work, priorities, and status.
  • Inspection: review progress at sprint boundaries and during standups.
  • Adaptation: change the plan when reality differs from assumptions.

Why the current controls still matter

Design review, lead code review, UAT, and release controls are not “anti-Agile.” They remain necessary governance and quality steps. Scrum makes them visible and time-boxed rather than hidden at the end of a long cycle.

Common misconceptions

  • “No documentation or design work.” Not true.
  • “We can skip review and quality gates.” Not true.
  • “The team should do whatever is urgent and ignore planning.” Not true.
  • “Scrum means no managers or no technical leadership.” Not true.

Lead questions to be ready to answer

  • What changes in the way work is organized and visible?
  • What stays the same in design, review, and UAT?
  • Why is the Sprint Goal important when work can still be urgent?
  • How do we use the board to support transparency and adaptation?

Teach the distinction

Agile Principle Transparency, inspection, and adaptation are general principles. Scrum Practice Sprints, Sprint Goals, and events are framework practices. Program Rule Our ADO states, approvals, 50/25/25 allocation, and five-business-day aging rule come from the Phased Sprint Delivery Plan—not from Scrum.

How it works here: Program management asks “Are we delivering agreed outcomes?” Team operations asks “How are we completing the work?” The October 1 change is visible, traceable sprint execution, not removal of governance or engineering controls.

Failure modes and coaching

  • Calling every local rule “Scrum” → name the source.
  • Treating a sprint as a deadline guarantee → teach forecast and adaptation.
  • Hiding review or UAT outside the sprint → put the work and evidence on the board.
  • Equating Agile with no documentation → explain useful transparency.

Scenario questions and self-check

  1. A request arrives mid-sprint: what is the team practice and what is the program rule?
  2. Why can UAT remain required in an Agile flow?
  3. Which question belongs to program governance?
  4. What does a visible board allow the team to inspect?
  5. What is the difference between a principle and a framework?
  6. What changes October 1?

Answer key: Document and route scope changes; quality controls remain; “Are we delivering agreed outcomes?” is program-level; the board exposes movement and risk; Agile is the mindset and Scrum is a framework; daily work, ceremonies, traceability, and board discipline become explicit.

Learning objectives

  • Explain what changes on October 1.
  • Recognize the new Scrum lifecycle and key events.
  • Map current work into backlog, refinement, sprint, review, and retrospective flow.

10–15 minute concept explanation

Historically, work flows from Design Review → Development → Code Review → UAT → Go Live. Scrum does not eliminate this; it makes the work visible in a shorter cadence with a backlog, board, and sprint goal.

Old model: wait until a big block of work is mostly complete, then review and validate.
New model: plan small slices of value, inspect progress often, and adapt based on evidence.

Examples from our environment

  • Design review remains a quality gate, but becomes part of the planned work and visible in the board.
  • Lead code review is part of the sprint flow, not an after-the-fact cleanup step.
  • UAT happens against the increment and remains required.
  • Go-live may occur before or after a sprint boundary, but the sprint should still show the work that was completed.

Hands-on exercise

Take a current project, defect, or enhancement and map it to the new lifecycle:

  1. What is the backlog item?
  2. What part is in refinement?
  3. What work is selected for the sprint?
  4. What board state changes during development and review?
  5. When does UAT or acceptance happen?
  6. What makes the work Done?

Key rules / takeaways

  • Backlog and board become the source of truth for work in progress.
  • Standups become board-driven instead of personal status reports.
  • Done still requires evidence: review, validation, and accurate board updates.
  • Scrum does not mean “no process”; it means visible and inspectable process.

Facilitator prompts and knowledge check

Ask: “What would a new board make visible?” and “Which control must remain?” Then have each learner answer: What is Agile? What is Scrum? What is our October 1 source of truth? Why do we still review and test? What is one behavior that changes tomorrow?

Takeaway: Use Scrum practices to make the local program model visible; do not rename program governance as generic Scrum.

Key takeaways

Scrum is a way to make planning, review, and learning visible inside a short work cycle. It is not a promise that we stop doing design, code review, UAT, or release governance; it is a way to integrate those controls into a healthier cadence.
