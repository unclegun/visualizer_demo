Session 2

Roles, Responsibilities, and Ownership

Why it matters Lead prep: 45–60 min Team session: 45–60 min
Why it matters: Without clarity about who owns priorities, who owns the technical solution, and how leaders facilitate rather than command, teams drift into confusion and silent rework.

Scrum roles and responsibilities

Product Owner

  • Owns product value and priority.
  • Defines outcomes and clarifies requirements.
  • Accepts work as it meets agreed value and criteria.

Scrum Master

  • Facilitates the process, not the work itself.
  • Helps remove impediments and improve the team’s effectiveness.
  • Protects the sprint and encourages healthy routines.

Developers

  • Own technical execution.
  • Estimate work and commit to sprint goals.
  • Deliver and validate the increment.

Stakeholders

  • Provide context, feedback, and acceptance decisions.
  • Do not direct the daily work of the team.
  • Engage during review and release conversations.

How this applies here

Our organization may not have perfect textbook roles. In a small team, the team lead may be both a developer and the functional facilitator. That is okay as long as responsibilities remain clear: the team owns delivery; the lead supports and coaches the process without becoming a command-and-control bottleneck.

Program context: The Program Manager protects contract scope, resources, and reporting. The Government Lead / TPOC sets macro priorities within approved scope. The Team Lead manages team-level execution, while developers own their stories and work items daily.

Technical lead vs Scrum Master

  • Technical lead: owns engineering quality, implementation approach, and review decisions.
  • Scrum Master: helps the team inspect and adapt its process and removes friction.
  • These are not the same job, even when the same person is doing both in a small team.

Facilitation vs command-and-control

  • Ask questions and surface tradeoffs rather than simply assign tasks.
  • Keep the Sprint Goal visible; do not chase every emergent request without discussion.
  • Coach the team to decide and own the work, while leadership still sets outcomes and priorities.

Learning objectives

  • Know who decides what matters most.
  • Know who decides how the work is implemented.
  • Know who accepts the work and how to escalate blockers.

Who does what?

QuestionTypical ownerHow it works in our environment
What work is most important?Government Lead / TPOCEpic- and sprint-level priorities are directed within approved contract scope; the customer and team shape exact sprint scope.
How should it be built?Developers + team leadImplementation decisions come from the team, with technical guidance.
Is the work acceptable?Stakeholders / customer / UAT ownerAcceptance happens through UAT and review.
How do we remove blockers?Team Lead → Government Lead / PMEscalate schedule, technical, scope, and dependency risks immediately rather than waiting for a status review.

Exercise: realistic responsibility scenarios

  1. A user requests an item not in the sprint plan. Who decides whether it changes priority?
  2. A developer disagrees with the technical approach. Who decides the final implementation path?
  3. A bug arises in production during the sprint. Who owns the urgent response and how is the Sprint Goal adjusted?

Key rules / takeaways

  • Prioritization is not the same as technical execution.
  • Team leads are still developers; they do not become a separate “boss” layer unless the process requires it.
  • Ownership should be explicit enough that blockers do not disappear into silence.

Key takeaways

Clear ownership reduces confusion. Scrum does not mean every person self-directs with no accountability; it means the team operates with visible roles, shared goals, and transparent decision-making.
